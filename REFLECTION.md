I found a lot of things annoying about this homework. The biggest thing was the amount of things I had to keep track of, especially because there were different specific requirements for every single page that it just became a massive headache to constantly make sure that requirements are satisfied. However, this was only so annoying because everything was done manually.

What I repeated by hand was the header, the nav, and the footer, nine times each, plus the whole `<head>` block with charset, viewport, description, and stylesheet links. They were all mostly the same but with subtle differences that gave me a brain aneurysm. Because I pasted the footer and then edited each copy, I ended up with five different footers across the site without noticing. My four family pages were the same story: `blue.css`, `fresh.css`, `hard.css`, and `soft.css` were byte-identical copies until I collapsed them into one `family.css`.

Moving files broke paths in ways I did not expect. My nav links were written as `/index.html`, which works at a domain root but silently breaks under a project subpath, and my family pager pointed at `/guide/soft.html`. Once the guide lived in `guide/`, every stylesheet and image needed `../`. `404.html` turned out to be the opposite problem: the host serves it for any bad URL, so relative paths break there and it needs root-absolute ones instead. Comments went stale too — `family.css` credited `global.css` for the pager's flex row, which stopped being true the moment I moved that rule into `site.css`.

As per the professor's lesson of doing this homework, I now understand the ease and convenience of using components that have tunable features, which makes it much easier to keep track of things and make changes. This homework had to have been crafted from the deepest pits of hell. Never again.
